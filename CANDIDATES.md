# Candidates — Ridiculous at First Glance, Real by the Second

One sentence, at most 200 characters, pitching a bizarre startup idea that
sounds absurd until you realise it would actually sell. Each candidate names
(a) the odd thing sold, (b) who pays, and (c) why the business holds together.

Scored 0–25 against the judge's criteria, in their published order (5 points
each): (1) pitches a bizarre startup idea; (2) genuinely bizarre and new, not
a familiar business with a strange adjective; (3) business logic holds — clear
why someone pays; (4) lands as a pitch — a turn from laughing to nodding;
(5) concrete and vivid — names the thing and the buyer, not abstractions.

## C1 — 196 characters — 25/25 — WINNER

> We sell believable one-star reviews to businesses with perfect five-star ratings, because shoppers distrust perfection and one honest flaw makes the rest of the praise — and the sale — believable.

Scores: (1) 5 — a startup selling bad reviews on purpose. (2) 5 — an
inversion nobody has heard before; the product itself is the joke.
(3) 5 — shoppers really do distrust flawless ratings, so one credible flaw
raises trust and conversion. (4) 5 — "wait, that would actually work" is the
whole sentence. (5) 5 — the thing (one-star reviews) and the buyer (perfectly
rated businesses) are both named.

## C2 — 191 characters — 22/25

> We sell your home Wi-Fi name to local shops, so neighbours see 'Dave's Pizza — 20% off tonight' every time they connect, because a name everyone reads daily is the cheapest billboard in town.

Scores: (1) 5, (2) 4 — novel placement, but advertising space is a familiar
business, (3) 4 — the reach logic is sound, though attention is assumed,
(4) 4, (5) 5.

## C3 — 177 characters — 23/25

> We hire strangers to greet you at arrivals holding your name on a sign, because impressing your in-laws with a fake fan club is cheaper than the wedding they expect you to fund.

Scores: (1) 5, (2) 4 — rent-a-crowd is a known shape, (3) 4 — status logic is
real but the wedding-funding line is doing most of the work, (4) 5, (5) 5.

## C4 — 179 characters — 24/25

> We sell a big red button that does nothing to stressed CEOs, because pressing it feels like taking control, and feeling in control is cheaper than a coach and faster than therapy.

Scores: (1) 5, (2) 5 — a placebo button as a product is genuinely new,
(3) 4 — the control-illusion psychology is real, but paying for a useless
object is a harder sell than C1's, (4) 5, (5) 5.

## C5 — 188 characters — 22/25

> We rent out professional arguers to settle family Christmas debates, because a neutral stranger's verdict ends the row in minutes and everyone can go back to pretending to like each other.

Scores: (1) 5, (2) 4 — rent-a-person sits close to the field's rent-a-mourner
angle, (3) 4, (4) 4, (5) 5.

## C6 — 186 characters — 24/25

> We supply a professional villain for your wedding — one rude guest everyone can unite against — because nothing bonds two families faster than a shared enemy, and we take the blame home.

Scores: (1) 5, (2) 5 — a hired villain is genuinely new, (3) 4 — bonding over
a shared enemy is real social psychology, but whether couples would pay for
it is less certain than C1's buyer, (4) 5, (5) 5.

## C7 — 158 characters — 21/25

> We rent people to stand in shop windows pretending to load, because shoppers stop, stare, and come inside to find out what the human buffering is waiting for.

Scores: (1) 5, (2) 5 — very new, (3) 3 — the logic is the weakest: staring
does not clearly convert into buying, (4) 4, (5) 4 — the buyer (shops) is
only implied.

## C8 — 153 characters — 20/25

> We sell recordings of library hush to open-plan offices, because workers pay for silence they can hear, and a hush track is cheaper than building a wall.

Scores: (1) 5, (2) 3 — white-noise and focus-sound products already exist, so
this is a familiar business with a strange adjective, (3) 4, (4) 3, (5) 5.

## Decision

C1 wins at 25/25: the strangest premise (selling bad reviews) with the
soundest logic (distrust of perfection is documented buyer behaviour), a
clean laugh-to-nod turn, and both the product and the payer named in one
sentence. C4 and C6 tie second at 24/25.
